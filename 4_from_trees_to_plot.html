Quantifying uncertainty in tree biomass · page 4

From 25 Trees to a Plot

What you are looking at. On page 3 one tree had a biomass uncertainty of about ±40 %. Here we add up 25 measured trees from a Miyawaki plot. Each tree is still ±40 %, yet the total is known to about ±12 %. Why? Random errors partly cancel when you add many trees: one tree comes out high, another low. But some errors are shared (the same wood-density table, the same equation for every tree), and those do not cancel. This page lets you watch both effects.

Why is the mean above the point total? The point total is 286 kg, but the mean of the draws comes out near 306 kg. The equation gives the median tree; the real tree is median × eε. With σ = 0.357, eε averages eσ²/2 = e0.064 ≈ 1.066, so on average each tree is 6.6 % heavier than the equation says (a log-normal effect: doubling adds more than halving removes). The median of the draws is the fairer "middle" to report; it stays near the point total.

Why the error shrinks

Per hectare

Field plots are small; reports are "per hectare". Dividing by the plot area and multiplying by 10,000 m² keeps the same percentage uncertainty, so the interval scales with the number.

Area = trees ÷ density. Edit either density or area; the other follows. If you change the number of trees away from 25, the total is scaled by (trees ÷ 25), assuming the 25 measured trees are typical.

Caution: this is an extrapolation. Scaling about 8 m² up to 10,000 m² multiplies by roughly 1,200. The interval shown is only the measurement + model uncertainty of these 25 trees. It says nothing about the sampling uncertainty: had you measured a different 8 m² of the same planting, you would have got a different total. Chave et al. (2004) found that sampling error alone is about 10 % for a 0.25 ha plot and about 5 % for a 1 ha plot; for a plot of a few square metres it is far larger. A per-hectare number from one tiny plot should be reported with that warning attached. Sanity check: at 3 trees per m² these 25 illustrative trees give roughly 340 t/ha, which is what a mature tropical rainforest carries, not a 5-year-old planting. Either the density is lower, the illustrative D and H are too generous, or the equation (tested on natural forest, D ≥ 5 cm) overestimates small crowded planted trees. A result that fails a sanity check is a finding to report, with the suspects named.

What to notice

  • Adding shrinks random error. Each tree is ±40 %, the 25-tree total is about ±12 %. High and low draws cancel. This is why foresters measure many trees instead of measuring one tree very carefully.
  • But not as fast as 1/√n. With 25 equal trees the CV would be 40/√25 = 8 %. Our trees are very unequal (the two biggest are a third of the total), so the "effective" number of trees is smaller than 25, and the curve in panel C sits above the dashed line.
  • Shared errors do not shrink. Flip the "independent per tree" switch: the curve drops further. In reality every tree of a species uses the same density value and every tree uses the same equation, so that part of the error is systematic and stays, however many trees you add. This becomes a floor when n is large.
  • Mean ≠ median for lopsided piles. The log-normal scatter pushes the mean about 6–7 % above the point total. Report the median and the 95 % interval.
  • Per-hectare numbers from tiny plots are extrapolations. The interval covers measurement and model error only; the sampling error of "which 8 m² did we pick" is extra and probably bigger.
