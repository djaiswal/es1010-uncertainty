Quantifying Uncertainty in Tree Biomass
Student Guide

Ecology & Environment 2026 · Deepak Jaiswal · plain-language edition, 1 November 2026

You measured a tree once. You put the numbers into an equation and got a biomass, a carbon content, and an amount of CO₂. This guide shows how to say, honestly, how sure you are about those numbers. It shows how to put an "error bar" on one tree and on the total of your 25 trees.

The short answer: one tree is uncertain by about a factor of two (its true biomass could be half the number, or double it). The total of 25 trees is uncertain by about ± 12 %. Most of that uncertainty comes from the equation, not from your tape measure.

How to use this guide

  • For the project: read Parts 4 to 7 and follow the checklist in 8.2. Each step of the checklist points back to the part that explains it.
  • For the exam: Parts 1 to 3 are the ideas. 8.3 has practice questions with answers. 8.4 is the glossary (list of words).
  • If statistics feels difficult: start at Part 1. Do not skip 1.4 (the √n rule) and 1.9 (how errors combine). Everything else in this guide is these two ideas, used again and again.
  • Interactive pages: four short web pages let you watch the methods work. Open them on a phone or laptop. No account is needed. Each part names its page.

Part 1 · Revision: the little statistics you need

You need only six ideas: the mean, the standard deviation, the standard error, the bell curve, the 95 % interval, and how errors combine. Each one is explained with the same example: the 24 height readings of tree #406. Interactive page: 1 · Dice to Bootstrap.

1.1 Sample and truth: why we never see the true value

The true height of tree #406 is one number. Nobody knows it. What we have is a sample: 24 groups of students each read a height, and the readings are different from each other (5.0 m, 7.3 m, 6.1 m, …). Statistics is the skill of saying something honest about the true value, using a sample whose values disagree with each other.

1.2 The mean: the centre of the pile

Add the values. Divide by how many there are.

mean  x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) ÷ n
Example with three readings: 5, 6, 7 mean = (5 + 6 + 7) ÷ 3 = 18 ÷ 3 = 6

For the 24 height readings of tree #406, the mean is 6.14 m. The mean is our best single guess of the true height.

1.3 The standard deviation (SD): how big the spread is

Readings 5, 6, 7 are close together: small spread. Readings 2, 6, 10 are far apart: large spread. Both sets have the same mean, 6. The standard deviation (short form SD, symbol s) is one number that measures the spread. Here is how to calculate it:

  1. Take each reading and subtract the mean. This gives the "difference from the mean".
  2. Square each difference (multiply it by itself). This makes all the differences positive, so they cannot cancel each other.
  3. Add the squares. Divide by n − 1 (one less than the number of readings).
  4. Take the square root.
SD  s = √[ Σ (xᵢ − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) ]

Read it as: "the square root of: the sum of the squared differences, divided by n − 1". xᵢ means "each reading in turn".

Example with readings 5, 6, 7 (mean 6, n = 3): differences from the mean: 5 − 6 = −1, 6 − 6 = 0, 7 − 6 = +1 squares: 1, 0, 1 sum of squares: 2 divide by n − 1 = 2: 1 square root: s = 1 Same steps with readings 2, 6, 10 (mean 6): differences: −4, 0, +4 squares: 16, 0, 16 sum: 32 ÷ 2 = 16 s = 4

So the first set has SD 1 and the second set has SD 4. The SD is roughly the typical distance of a reading from the mean. For the 24 heights of tree #406, s = 0.68 m. Read it like this: one reading, picked at random, is usually about 0.68 m away from the mean. A small SD means the readers agree with each other. A large SD means they do not.

The coefficient of variation (short form CV) is the same spread written as a percentage of the mean: CV = s ÷ x̄. For the heights, CV = 0.68 ÷ 6.14 = 11 %. The CV lets you compare the spread of heights (in metres) with the spread of diameters (in centimetres), because a percentage has no unit. In this guide and on the web pages, a spread written as a percentage is always a CV. An SD always carries a unit (m, cm, kg). So "CV 10 %" for a 6.0 m tree means SD = 0.6 m.

1.4 The standard error (SE): how sure we are about the mean

The SD tells you how much one reading can differ from another. But we do not report one reading. We report the mean of 24 readings. The mean is much more stable than one reading, because high readings and low readings partly cancel each other. The rule:

SE = s ÷ √n

For tree #406: SE = 0.68 ÷ √24 = 0.68 ÷ 4.9 = 0.14 m. This is the standard error of the mean. It is the SD that the mean itself would have, if many different groups of 24 students measured the tree and we compared their means.

Notice: doubling the number of readings does not halve the error. To halve the error you need four times as many readings, because √4 = 2. This √n rule is the single most important formula in this guide. It returns in Part 7 when we add 25 trees.

1.5 The bell curve and the 68–95 rule

When many small, independent causes push a measurement up or down, the pile of readings takes a bell shape: most readings near the middle, fewer far away, the same on both sides. This shape is called the normal distribution or the bell curve. For a bell curve:

  • about 68 % of the values lie within ± 1 SD of the mean,
  • about 95 % lie within ± 2 SD (more exactly ± 1.96 SD),
  • about 99.7 % lie within ± 3 SD.

You do not need the formula of the curve. You need the 95 % rule, because that is what a 95 % interval is built from.

1.6 The 95 % confidence interval (CI)

There are two ways to get it. They agree when the pile is bell-shaped.

Formula way: mean ± 1.96 × SE. For tree #406: 6.14 ± 1.96 × 0.14, which gives 5.87 to 6.41 m.

Counting way (no formula): make a long list of possible values of the mean (Part 2 shows how). Sort the list. Cut off the lowest 2.5 % and the highest 2.5 %. What remains, the middle 95 %, is the interval. The counting way also works when the pile is not bell-shaped, and biomass piles never are.

How to read a 95 % CI: if we repeated the whole exercise many times, about 95 of every 100 intervals built this way would contain the true value. It is a statement about the method, not a promise about this one tree.

1.7 Piles that are not symmetric: the log-normal

Biomass errors multiply; they do not add. A tree is 1.4 times heavier than predicted, or 1.4 times lighter. It is not "+ 5 kg" or "− 5 kg". A pile of multiplying errors is squeezed on the left and stretched on the right: a tree cannot be 150 % too light (its weight would be below zero), but it can easily be 150 % too heavy. Such a pile is called log-normal.

Taking the logarithm turns "times" into "plus" and makes the pile bell-shaped again. That is why the Chave equation is fitted on log axes, and why its scatter σ = 0.357 is given in log units. In plain numbers: e0.357 = 1.43, so "σ = 0.357" means "about ± 40 %".

1.8 Random error and systematic error

  • Random error goes both ways around the truth, with no pattern. Sometimes too high, sometimes too low. Averaging many readings makes it smaller (by √n).
  • Systematic error (also called bias) pushes the same way every time. Example: a bathroom scale that always reads 2 kg too heavy. Averaging does not make it smaller. Only a better instrument, a better equation, or a correction removes it.

In this guide, the scatter of the equation and the differences between readers are random. The wood-density value from a table, the carbon fraction, and the equation's tendency to read 5 % too high are systematic.

1.9 How errors combine

When a result is calculated from several uncertain inputs, the uncertainties do not simply add up. For independent random errors, the rule is: square each one, add the squares, take the square root.

total error = √( error₁² + error₂² + error₃² + … )
Example: 37 % (equation) and 10 % (height) and 5 % (diameter) √(37² + 10² + 5²) = √(1369 + 100 + 25) = √1494 ≈ 38.6 % (not 37 + 10 + 5 = 52 %)

The biggest term decides the result almost alone. The small terms almost disappear.

Three more rules you will use:

  • Multiplying or dividing: the percentage errors combine by the square-add-root rule above.
  • Powers: multiply the percentage error by the exponent. D² has twice the percentage error of D. A 3 % error in D becomes a 6 % error in D².
  • Adding independent quantities (25 trees): the absolute errors (in kg) combine by the square-add-root rule. This is exactly why the percentage error of a total gets smaller like 1 ÷ √n.

These rules are all you need to check a computer result by hand. The computer methods in Part 2 do the same job without any formula, and without assuming that errors are independent or bell-shaped.

Part 2 · Two computer methods that replace formulas: bootstrap and Monte Carlo

Both methods rest on one idea: probability means "how often, in the long run". If you can repeat something many times, or make a computer repeat it, you can count how often each outcome happens instead of calculating it with a formula. Interactive page: 1 · Dice to Bootstrap.

2.1 The picture to keep in mind: a die, rolled many times

Roll a fair die 10,000 times. Each face comes up about 1,667 times, never exactly. Watch the running average (the mean of all rolls so far). After 10 rolls it may be 2.9 or 4.1. After 10,000 rolls it sits within a few hundredths of 3.5. The spread of the running average gets smaller like 1 ÷ √n, the rule from 1.4.

Now roll two dice each time and write down their average. The pile of averages is narrower: SD 1.21 instead of 1.71. Roll ten dice and average them: narrower still, SD 0.54 = 1.71 ÷ √10. Nothing about the dice changed. Averaging did the work.

Now think of a measurement as a roll of a die that you did not design. The die is the whole measuring process: the person, the tape, the angle reader, the leaves in the way, the light. One group's reading of tree #406 (say 6.1 m) is one roll. The faces of the die are all the heights that this process could produce. We have 24 rolls, one per group. Can we learn the shape of the die from only 24 rolls?

2.2 Bootstrap: when you have data but no formula

Yes, by re-rolling the 24 rolls we already have. This is the bootstrap (Efron, 1979; the "bag of chips" picture is from Efron & Tibshirani, 1993).

  1. Bag. Write each of the 24 height readings on a chip. Put the chips in a bag.
  2. Draw. Pull out one chip, write down its value, put it back. Repeat 24 times. You now have a pretend sample of 24 readings. Some original readings appear twice or three times. Some do not appear at all.
  3. Calculate. Take the mean of the pretend sample.
  4. Repeat. Do steps 2 and 3 ten thousand times. You get 10,000 pretend means.
  5. Read off. The SD of the 10,000 pretend means is the standard error. Sort them and cut 2.5 % off each end: the middle 95 % is the confidence interval.

For tree #406 (24 readings, mean 6.14 m, SD 0.68 m) the bootstrap gives SE ≈ 0.14 m and a 95 % interval of about 5.88 to 6.40 m. The formula s ÷ √n also gives 0.14 m. They agree. No formula was needed, only counting.

Why put the chip back? We are pretending that the 24 chips are the die. A die can show the same face twice in a row. So a chip must be allowed to come out twice. If we did not put the chips back, every pretend sample would be the same 24 values in a different order, and every pretend mean would be exactly 6.14 m. We would learn nothing.

Assumptions the bootstrap makes (write them in your report)

  • The 24 readings are a fair sample of what the measuring process can produce. If every group used the same wrong eye height, the bootstrap cannot see that bias.
  • The readings are independent of each other. Groups did not copy each other's numbers.
  • 24 readings are enough to sketch the shape of the die. With 5 readings the sketch is rough. With 3 it is useless.
  • The bootstrap tells you about precision (spread), never about accuracy (bias). A method that always gives the same wrong answer will bootstrap to a tiny interval around that wrong answer.

2.3 Monte Carlo: when you know the dice and want the result

The bootstrap learns the shape of a die from data. Monte Carlo goes the other way: you already know (or assume) the shape of each die, and you want to know how their combination behaves. The name comes from the Monte Carlo casino. The method was invented in the 1940s by physicists whose formulas had become too hard to solve.

For biomass the "dice" are the uncertain inputs: height H, diameter D, wood density ρ, the two fitted numbers of the equation, and the equation's leftover scatter ε. The recipe:

  1. Roll every die once. Pick one possible H, one D, one ρ, one pair (a, b), and one ε, each from its own range of possible values.
  2. Calculate. AGB = a × (ρ × D² × H)b × eε.
  3. Write the answer down.
  4. Repeat 20,000 times.
  5. Read off. The middle of the pile (the median) is the estimate. The SD or CV is the uncertainty. The middle 95 % of the pile is the interval.

Where do the dice come from? From data when you have it (the bootstrap of 24 readings gives the H die). From published papers when you do not (σ = 0.357 for the scatter; four published values for ρ). From a best guess when neither exists (± 20 % for a height measured only once). Part 6 lists each die for the Chave equation.

2.5 How many repeats?

A Monte Carlo result has its own small uncertainty, which gets smaller like 1 ÷ √N, where N is the number of repeats. With N = 10,000 the ends of the 95 % interval are known to about ± 1 %. A laptop does 20,000 repeats of the biomass equation in less than a second, so N is never the problem. Use at least 5,000. Use 20,000 for the report.

Part 3 · Where a biomass equation comes from, and the two ways it can be wrong

You cannot weigh a living tree. So you predict its weight from its shape. Every such prediction carries two separate uncertainties from the equation itself: the fitted numbers could be slightly off (a small effect), and real trees scatter around any line (a large effect). Interactive page: 2 · How an Equation Is Born.

3.1 Cut, dry, weigh

An allometric equation predicts something hard to measure (dry weight) from things that are easy to measure (diameter D, height H, wood density ρ). To build one, researchers:

  1. Choose trees of many sizes and species.
  2. Measure D and H while the tree is standing. Take a piece of wood to measure ρ.
  3. Cut the tree down. Cut it into pieces. Weigh everything above the ground: trunk, branches, leaves.
  4. Dry small pieces in an oven to find out how much of the fresh weight was water. Water is not biomass.
  5. Plot dry weight against size and fit a curve.

Chave et al. (2014) collected 4,004 such trees from 58 sites across the tropics, from 5 cm saplings to giants of 100 tonnes, and fitted one equation to all of them:

AGB = 0.0673 × (ρ × D² × H)0.976

AGB is in kg of oven-dry matter, ρ in g/cm³, D in cm, H in m. The bracket ρ × D² × H is roughly the mass of a cylinder as wide and as tall as the tree. A real tree weighs about 7 % of that cylinder, because it gets thinner towards the top and splits into branches. The two numbers 0.0673 and 0.976 are not theory. They are the position and slope of a straight line fitted through 4,004 points on log–log axes (logs turn "times" into "plus" and make the scatter bell-shaped; see 1.7).

3.2 Doubt 1: where exactly is the line? (parameter uncertainty)

Cut down a different 4,004 trees and fit again, and you get slightly different numbers, say 0.0681 and 0.975. The fitted numbers (the parameters) are themselves estimates, each with its own standard error. On the interactive page, fitting a new set of 50 trees moves the line a lot. Fitting a new set of 4,000 trees hardly moves it. With 4,004 trees behind it, the Chave line is fixed to well under 1 % for a tree the size of ours.

Two properties of this doubt:

  • It is systematic. If the true slope is 0.980 rather than 0.976, every tree you calculate is off in the same direction. Measuring more of your own trees does not cancel it.
  • It is small here. In this project we carry it as a shared ± 1 % and move on.

3.3 Doubt 2: real trees are not on the line (scatter)

Even with a perfect line, real trees land above or below it. Two trees with the same D, H and ρ do not weigh the same. One has more branches. One lost its top years ago. The vertical spread of the cloud of points around the line is called the residual scatter, written σ (sigma). For the Chave equation, σ = 0.357 in natural-log units.

What 0.357 means in plain numbers: e0.357 = 1.43. So for one tree, the true weight is usually (68 % of the time) between 0.7 and 1.4 times the prediction, and 95 % of the time between 0.5 and 2.0 times the prediction. For tree #406, predicted at 13.6 kg, that is roughly 7 to 28 kg. This is by far the biggest single uncertainty in the whole project.

Two properties, the opposite of Doubt 1:

  • It is random. Each tree lands above or below the line independently of the others. Add 25 trees, and the highs and lows partly cancel (Part 7).
  • It does not get smaller when more trees are cut down to build the equation. It belongs to the trees, not to the sample size. A million cut-down trees would fix the line perfectly, and the cloud around it would be exactly as wide.

3.5 Two more things the paper admits

  • Bias. Averaged over the 58 sites, the equation guesses about 5 % too heavy for ordinary trees and about 20 % too light for giants over 30 tonnes. A bias is systematic: it stays however many trees we measure. For honesty we name it. We do not correct for it, because we do not know whether it applies to young planted trees.
  • Tested range. Every one of the 4,004 trees had D ≥ 5 cm and grew in natural forest. Our 5-year-old, densely planted Miyawaki trees sit right at that edge, and the paper's smallest trees had its largest errors. Using an equation at the edge of its data is allowed. Pretending that the only error is σ is not. Say so in the report.

3.6 The sentence to remember

Chave et al. (2014) write that their method "provides estimates – not direct measurements – and model error should always be carefully examined and propagated through to the quantity of interest." "Propagated through" means: carried along into the final answer. Parts 5 to 7 do exactly that.

Part 4 · One tree: are the numbers you wrote down right?

Your field sheet says: tree #406, circumference C = 25.1 cm, so D = 8.0 cm; H = 6.0 m. Before using any equation, ask: if a classmate measured the same tree five minutes later, would they write down the same numbers? If not, by how much would they differ? That difference is your input uncertainty. It has to be put into words and then into a number.

4.1 Three ways to put a number on "not sure"

(a) Several people measured it. Tree #406 was measured by all 24 groups. The heights ran from 5.0 to 7.3 m (mean 6.14 m, SD 0.68 m, CV 11 %). The diameters ran from 7.5 to 8.5 cm (mean 7.98 cm, SD 0.26 cm, CV 3 %). The SD across people is the die for that measurement. Bootstrap it (2.2). This is the best case. It is why the project has reference trees measured by every team.

(b) You measured it once, but you know your instrument and your method. Then build the uncertainty from its parts. Height by tape and angle: H = d × tan θ + eye height, where d is the distance to the tree and θ is the angle to the top. With d = 10 m and θ = 30°: an angle error of 1° changes H by about 0.24 m (4 %); a distance error of 0.3 m changes H by 0.17 m (3 %); choosing a different twig as "the top" can change H by 0.5 m (8 %). Combine by square-add-root: √(4² + 3² + 8²) ≈ 9 to 10 % for one careful reading. Diameter by tape: reading to 1 mm on an 8 cm trunk is 1 %; holding the tape 10 cm higher or lower on a tapering trunk adds 1 to 2 %; an oval trunk adds a few percent. Together 2 to 3 %.

(c) You have nothing but experience: a best guess. State it and defend it. Reasonable default values for this project, which we use in Parts 6 and 7 whenever repeat readings are missing:

Height H, tape and angle, measured once± 20 %10 % of Hmatches the 11 % spread seen across 24 groups; height is measured from a distance and calculated

A ± 20 % range that covers 95 % of readings corresponds to an SD of about 10 % (1.5: 95 % is ± 1.96 SD). A best guess is not cheating. It is an assumption, written down, that a reader can question and that you can replace with data later.

4.2 Height is much harder to measure than diameter

Diameter is a contact measurement: you touch the trunk, make one decision (where is 1.3 m?), and pull the tape tight. Different people get almost the same number. Height is measured from a distance and then calculated: you never touch the top, you guess which twig is the top in a thick canopy, you read an angle, you measure a distance with a tape, and you add your eye height. Each step has its own error, and the tan θ in the formula makes angle errors bigger. Typical result: 2 to 3 % spread in D, 10 to 20 % in H.

But D is squared in the equation, so a 3 % error in D becomes a 6 % error in AGB, while a 10 % error in H stays 10 %. Even so, height remains the larger field error.

4.3 Random or systematic? Ask for each input

  • A different reader each time, a different standing position, a different twig chosen as the top: random. Averages out over many trees.
  • Everyone used the same stick with the mark at 1.25 m instead of 1.30 m; everyone added an eye height 10 cm too large; the tape counts bark as wood; the angle method under-reads leaning trees; the true top is hidden in a thick canopy: systematic. Averages out over nothing.

For a single tree the difference hardly matters: both kinds make the interval wider. For 25 trees (Part 7) it matters a great deal.

4.5 What "error in the inputs" means for the result

The Chave equation needs three inputs: D, H and ρ. We measured two of them (with error) and looked the third one up (with spread). The equation then makes each error bigger or smaller: D is squared (× 2), H and ρ enter with the power 0.976 (× 1). So, in percent, an error of 2.5 % in D, 10 % in H and 10 % in ρ becomes about √((2 × 2.5)² + 10² + 10²) = √(25 + 100 + 100) ≈ 15 % in AGB, before the scatter of the equation is added. Part 5 lets the computer do this arithmetic by rolling dice.

Part 5 · Bootstrap and Monte Carlo applied to one tree

For tree #406 the honest answer is AGB ≈ 14 kg with a 95 % interval of 6.4 to 29 kg. That is a factor of two each way. About 92 % of that spread comes from the scatter of the equation. Interactive page: 3 · Monte Carlo, One Tree.

5.1 Step 1: turn the 24 readings into dice (bootstrap)

From the 24 group readings: mean H = 6.14 m, bootstrap SE 0.14 m, 95 % interval 5.88 to 6.40 m; mean D = 7.98 cm, SE 0.05 cm. Two different things are now available, and you must choose the right one:

  • If you want the AGB of tree #406 as the class knows it (all 24 readings pooled), the H die is narrow: SD 0.14 m (2 %). The class mean is well known.
  • If you want the AGB as one group would report it (one reading), the H die is the spread of single readings: SD 0.68 m (11 %). This is the die for your own 25 trees, each measured once.

This guide uses the second one, because it is your project's situation. We round it to the default best guess of 10 %.

Now notice what the arithmetic says: a perfect tape and a perfect height instrument (rows H and D set to zero) would only reduce 40 % to √(37² + 10² + 1²) ≈ 38 %. Converted: carbon = 0.47 × AGB → 6.4 kg (95 % interval 3.0 to 13.6); CO₂ = 3.67 × C → 23 kg (11 to 50). Note that we held 0.47 fixed; adding its ± 5 % would make the interval slightly wider.

5.4 Why the median, not the mean

The pile is not symmetric (1.7). Its median, 13.5 kg, sits at the plain-equation answer of 13.6 kg. Its mean is about 6 % higher, because the factor eε with σ = 0.357 has an average of eσ²/2 = 1.066, not 1. (A factor of 2 adds 100 %; a factor of 0.5 removes only 50 %; so on average the factor adds a little.) Statisticians argue about which to report: the mean is the expected weight; the median is the typical tree. For this course: report the median and the 95 % interval, and say which you used.

5.5 How to write the result

Wrong: "AGB = 13.608 kg." Four digits after the point, claimed from a tape, a height reading and an equation with 40 % scatter.

Right: "AGB ≈ 14 kg (95 % interval 6.4 to 29 kg). Interval from 20,000 Monte Carlo draws, carrying the group-to-group spread in H (10 %) and D (2.5 %), a 10 % log-normal spread in the database wood density, 1 % on the fitted parameters, and the published scatter of the equation (σ = 0.357). Most of the uncertainty comes from the scatter of the equation. Carbon fraction fixed at 0.47."

A number without an uncertainty is not a measurement. It is an opinion.

Part 6 · The Chave (2014) equation, term by term

The equation has five uncertain terms (a, b, ρ, D, H) plus the scatter ε. The original paper gives you a number for ε and for the bias. It does not give you numbers for the others. So you must bring them from elsewhere, or make a best guess, and say which you did.

6.1 The general form

Written the way it was fitted, on the log scale:

ln(AGB) = ln a + b × ln(ρ × D² × H) + ε,    where ε follows a bell curve with mean 0 and SD σ

and back on the ordinary scale:

AGB = a × (ρ × D² × H)b × eε

with a = 0.0673, b = 0.976, σ = 0.357 (Chave et al. 2014, Model 4, n = 4,004 trees, 58 sites). The term eε is the part most people forget. Leaving it out gives a single number. Keeping it gives the honest spread.

6.3 The version without height, for trees whose top you could not see

Chave et al. (2014) also fitted a version without H. It uses a climate index E (from the site's climate) in place of height:

ln(AGB) = −1.803 − 0.976 E + 0.976 ln ρ + 2.673 ln D − 0.0299 (ln D)² + ε

It has more fitted numbers (each with its own unprinted standard error) and a slightly larger scatter. The same logic applies: each fitted number is a die, E is a die (it depends on how well the climate maps describe Palakkad), and ε is the big die. If you use it, say so, and expect a wider interval, not a narrower one. Taking height out of the equation does not remove the uncertainty that height carried. It moves that uncertainty into the scatter.

6.4 A rule for every missing number

When the paper does not give an uncertainty for a term, do the following in order, and write down which step you reached:

  1. Measure it yourself (repeat readings, bootstrap). Best.
  2. Find it in another paper that measured the same thing (Chave et al. 2004 for D and H errors; the wood density database for ρ).
  3. Make a best guess, a little on the pessimistic side (± 20 % for a height measured once, ± 5 % for a diameter). Then test whether the final answer changes much if you double the guess. If it does not, the guess does not matter. If it does, say that it does.

What you may never do is set a term's uncertainty to zero because nobody gave you a number.

Every single tree has the same 40 % CV, because the scatter of the equation is the biggest source and it is the same for every tree. The total has 12 %. Nothing was measured better. The highs and lows of 25 independent scatters partly cancelled each other.

7.2 Why the total is better than any single tree

Three things to read off the curve:

  1. It falls like 1 ÷ √n at first. Two trees: 32 %. Four trees: 25 %. Nine trees: 15 %. Averaging works exactly as the dice in 2.1 promised.
  2. It stays above the 40 % ÷ √n line. That line assumes 25 trees of equal size. Ours range from 1.2 kg to 56 kg, and the largest tree alone is a fifth of the total. In effect, the plot behaves like only about 12 equal trees, not 25. Adding the two biggest trees last (n = 24 and 25) even pushes the CV back up a little. Rule: the error of a total is decided by the few trees that make up most of it. Measure the big trees most carefully.
  3. It will not go to zero. The shared dice (ρ per species, the equation's parameters, the carbon fraction, the + 5 % bias) do not cancel between trees. Switch off the scatter and the reading errors completely, and about 4 to 5 % remains with 13 species; with a single species it would be about 10 %. More trees never remove a systematic error.

7.3 Median, mean and the point total

The point total (every tree calculated with the plain equation) is 286 kg. The Monte Carlo median is 303 kg and the mean 306 kg. The 6 % gap is the log-normal effect of 5.4: a sum of 25 non-symmetric piles is itself almost bell-shaped, so its median moves up to meet its mean, eσ²/2 = 1.066 above the plain equation. All three values sit comfortably inside the 244 to 387 kg interval. Report the median with the interval, and mention the point total so that a reader can check it on a calculator.

7.4 Dividing by an area: per hectare

The lecture gives a typical Miyawaki planting density of about 3 trees per m², so 25 trees occupy roughly 8.3 m² (an assumption; replace it with your plot's measured area). Dividing by an area does not change the percentage uncertainty. It only changes the unit:

Two warnings belong next to that table in any report:

  • Going from 8 m² to a hectare is a big jump (a multiplication by about 1,200). The interval above carries only the measurement and equation uncertainty. It does not include the sampling uncertainty: which 8 m² you happened to measure. Chave et al. (2004) found that sampling error alone is about 10 % for a 0.25 ha plot and 5 % for 1 ha, and far larger for a few square metres. Pooling all 24 groups (600 trees, about 200 m²) is the honest way to approach a per-hectare figure.
  • Check whether the number makes sense. 300 to 450 t/ha of above-ground biomass is what a fully grown tropical rainforest carries, not a 5-year-old planting. Either the density assumption is too high, or the example D and H are too large, or the equation (tested on natural forest, D ≥ 5 cm) gives too-high values for small, crowded, planted trees. A result that fails this check is a finding, not a failure: write it down and name the possible reasons. This is the kind of claim the AI-audit session taught you to test.

8.3 Practice questions (answers in green)

  1. Twenty-four readings of a height have mean 6.0 m and SD 0.6 m. What is the standard error of the mean, and the 95 % interval? [SE = 0.6 ÷ √24 = 0.12 m; interval 6.0 ± 1.96 × 0.12 = 5.76 to 6.24 m]
  2. In the bootstrap, why are chips put back into the bag? [The 24 chips stand in for the whole process (the die). The same value must be allowed to appear twice. Otherwise every pretend sample is the same set of values with the same mean.]
  3. A tree's AGB has independent uncertainties of 37 % (equation), 10 % (H), 6 % (D²) and 10 % (ρ). What is the combined CV? What if the height were known exactly? [√(37² + 10² + 6² + 10²) ≈ 40 %; without H, √(37² + 6² + 10²) ≈ 39 %. Careful field work barely helps for one tree.]
  4. σ = 0.357 on the log scale. Between which factors does a single tree's true weight lie 95 % of the time? [e± 1.96 × 0.357 = 0.50 to 2.0 times the prediction]
  5. Name the two kinds of equation uncertainty. Which one gets smaller when more trees are cut down to build the equation? Which one gets smaller when more of our own trees are added together? [Parameter uncertainty: smaller with more cut-down trees, not with adding ours (systematic). Scatter: never smaller with more cut-down trees, partly cancels when ours are added (random).]
  6. 25 equal trees, each with 40 % random uncertainty: what is the CV of the total? Why is the real figure for our plot higher? [40 ÷ √25 = 8 %. Our trees are unequal (effective number about 12) and some errors are shared.]
  7. The D of a tree is 3 % uncertain. How uncertain is D²? [About 6 %: a power multiplies the percentage error by the exponent.]
  8. Give two systematic errors in this project and explain why measuring 1,000 trees would not remove them. [Database wood density; carbon fraction; the equation's + 5 % bias; a stick marked at the wrong height. Each pushes every tree the same way, so the average keeps the push.]
  9. Why does the Monte Carlo mean of a single tree sit above the plain-equation value? [Multiplying by eε, with ε bell-shaped around 0, gives an average factor of eσ²/2 = 1.066. The pile is not symmetric. The median matches the plain value.]
  10. A report says "CO₂ stored = 23.451 kg". Rewrite it correctly. ["CO₂ ≈ 23 kg (95 % interval 11 to 50 kg), from 20,000 Monte Carlo draws; most of the uncertainty comes from the scatter of the equation; carbon fraction fixed at 0.47."]
