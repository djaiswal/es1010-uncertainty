How an Equation Is Born

Where the biomass formula comes from, and the two different ways it can be wrong.

seed 2026 (same story every time you open the page)

What you are looking at

To weigh a standing tree we use an equation. The one in this course (Chave et al. 2014) is

AGB [kg] = 0.0673 × (ρ · D² · H)0.976

AGB is the above-ground biomass, ρ the wood density (g/cm³), D the trunk diameter (cm) and H the height (m). Nobody was handed this equation. Researchers felled 4,004 trees in 58 tropical forests, dried them, put them on a scale, and fitted a straight line through the data on log paper. Panel A lets you repeat that harvest with any number of trees. Panels B and C show the two kinds of doubt that remain even after the fit: where exactly the line is, and how far real trees sit from it. For tree #406 (D = 8.0 cm, H = 6.0 m, ρ = 0.60) the size variable is ρD²H = 230.4 and the equation predicts 13.6 kg.

How the fit works: take the natural log of every x and every weight, then find the straight line ln(AGB) = ln(a) + b·ln(x) that makes the sum of squared vertical distances smallest ("least squares"). The slope is b; the intercept gives a. The fitted numbers never come out exactly 0.0673 and 0.976, because the trees scatter. The more trees, the closer.

Try n = 20, then 4,000. With 20 trees the line can tilt visibly; with 4,000 it is pinned down.

Panel B

Uncertainty 1: where exactly is the line? (parameter uncertainty)

Suppose another team went to the forest and felled n different trees. They would get a slightly different line. Press the button to harvest again and again: the lines pile up into a fan. The width of the fan is the uncertainty of a and b. It depends on n, the number of trees harvested, and on nothing else.

The orange tile is what matters for our tree: by how many percent does the predicted weight of #406 move from one harvest to the next? Set n = 50 and refit 50 times, then n = 4,000 and refit 50 times. The percentage drops from several percent to well under 1 %. With the real 4,004 trees this doubt is about 1 %, the value used on the other pages.

Panel C

Uncertainty 2: trees do not sit on the line (residual scatter)

Even if we knew the line perfectly, a single tree is not on it. Two trees with the same ρ·D²·H can weigh quite different amounts: one is hollow, one has a heavy crown. The vertical spread of the dots around the line is σ = 0.357 on the log scale, which means a factor of about 1.4 either way for a typical tree, and a factor of 2 for the extremes (95 %). Press Play to fell trees of exactly the size of #406 and see where their weights land.

This band does not shrink when more trees are harvested. Harvesting 40,000 trees instead of 4,000 would pin the line down even better, but each new tree would still sit 0.7× to 1.4× away from it. The scatter is a property of trees, not of our sample size.

What does shrink the band is averaging. Move the "average of m trees" slider: if you report the average weight of m trees of this size, the band narrows by √m, because the hollow trees and the heavy-crowned trees cancel partly. For one tree the doubt is ±40 %; for the average of 100 such trees it is ±4 %.

Two kinds of doubt, side by side

Parameter uncertainty (Panel B)

  • Systematic. If the line is 1 % too high, it is 1 % too high for every tree in the plantation. The errors do not cancel across trees.
  • Same for every tree. It belongs to the equation, not to the tree.
  • Tiny with 4,004 trees: under 1 % on the predicted weight. It would be large if the equation had been built from 50 trees.

Residual scatter (Panel C)

  • Random. One tree is heavier than the line says, the next is lighter. The direction differs from tree to tree.
  • Large for one tree: about ±40 % (95 % range 0.5× to 2×).
  • Partly averages out over many trees: divide by √m. For a plantation of 400 trees it is about ±2 % on the total. It never shrinks by harvesting more trees for the equation.

What to notice

  1. An equation is a fitted line, not a law of nature. Its numbers 0.0673 and 0.976 came out of a particular harvest and would be slightly different with another.
  2. The fan in Panel B closes as n grows, roughly as 1/√n: 100× more trees, 10× narrower fan. Same rule as on page 1.
  3. The band in Panel C never closes. It is the single largest source of doubt for one tree (CV 37 %), bigger than the height, diameter and density errors together.
  4. Systematic vs random matters when you add trees up. For a whole plantation the random part shrinks and the systematic part remains. On the final page we will add these up for 1,000 trees.
