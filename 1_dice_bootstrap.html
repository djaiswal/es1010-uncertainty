Dice to Bootstrap

From a single die to the uncertainty of a tree measurement, in three steps.

What you are looking at

Three experiments, each with its own Play, Step and Reset. Panel A rolls one die and shows that a probability is simply what happens in the long run. Panel B averages several dice at once and shows that averages wobble less than single rolls, by a predictable amount. Panel C replaces the die by a bag of 24 real height readings of tree #406 and uses the same idea, called the bootstrap, to find out how uncertain the average of those 24 readings is. Press Play at slow speed first; press Fast-forward when you have understood one step.

Probability is a long-run frequency

A fair die shows each face with probability 1/6. You cannot see 1/6 in one roll. You see it after thousands of rolls, when every bar settles near the dashed line and the running mean settles at 3.5.

Panel B

Averages behave better than single rolls

Now roll k dice at once and record only their average. One die gives a flat spread from 1 to 6. The average of 25 dice almost never leaves 3 to 4. The spread (SD) shrinks as 1/√k. This is the single most useful fact in this course.

SD = standard deviation, the typical distance from the mean. CV = coefficient of variation, the SD as a percentage of the mean. "95 % interval" = from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile: 95 out of 100 rolls land inside it.

Try each k with Fast-forward. The two highlighted numbers agree to about two decimals every time. The formula is not a coincidence: averaging k independent things divides the SD by √k.

Panel C

The bootstrap: pull from the bag, with replacement

Twenty-four students each measured the height of tree #406. Their 24 readings sit in the bag. The average is 6.14 m. How sure can we be of that average? We cannot re-measure 10,000 times. So we pretend: draw 24 chips from the bag with replacement (put each chip back before the next draw), compute the mean of this pretend sample, and repeat. The spread of all those pretend means tells us the uncertainty of the real mean.

The SD of the bootstrap means is called the standard error (SE) of the mean. It says how much the average of 24 readings would move if 24 other students measured the same tree. After a few thousand resamples it matches the textbook formula s/√24 closely.

Why with replacement?

In Panel A the die has six faces and we rolled it as often as we liked. In the bootstrap the 24 chips stand in for the die: they are our best picture of "what a height reading of this tree can be". A die can show the same face twice in a row, so a chip must be allowed to come out twice too. Putting each chip back keeps all 24 faces available for every draw. Without replacement, every pretend sample would be exactly the original 24 readings in a different order, with exactly the same mean, and the histogram would be one single bar: no information about uncertainty at all.

What to notice

  1. Probability needs many trials. After 10 rolls the bars in Panel A are a mess. After 10,000 they are flat. "1/6" describes the long run, not the next roll.
  2. The 1/√n rule. In Panel A the running mean wobbles by about 1.71/√n. In Panel B the SD of an average of k dice is 1.71/√k. In Panel C the SE of the mean of 24 readings is s/√24. Same rule, three disguises.
  3. The bootstrap gives the SE without any formula. It works for means, for medians, for a biomass equation, for anything. We will use it for the whole tree in later pages.
  4. Height vs diameter. Switch the bag. The 24 diameter readings agree much better (CV of the mean below 1 %) than the heights (about 2 %). Measuring height with a clinometer is the shakier job.
