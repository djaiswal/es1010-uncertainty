Before we start

Revisiting probability using a die and a coin

Probability is a number between 0 and 1. It says how likely a result is. 0 means "never". 1 means "always". 1/2 means "half of the time". When every result has the same chance, the rule is simple:

Probability of a result = number of ways to get that result ÷ total number of possible results

Words used on this page

The words, in the order you will meet them

Read this once now, and come back whenever a word is unclear. Every word is explained with the same tiny example: three readings, 5, 6 and 7. A later section, "Simple calculations", works out all the numbers by hand.

1 · The data

Reading
One measured number. Also called a value or an observation. Example: one student measures the tree and writes 6.4 m. That is one reading.
DBH
Diameter at breast height. The thickness of the trunk, measured 1.3 m above the ground, in cm.
n
The number of readings. For 5, 6, 7 n is 3. For 2, 6, 10 n is also 3.

2 · The middle of the data

Mean
The average. Add all the readings, then divide by n. (5 + 6 + 7) ÷ 3 = 18 ÷ 3 = 6. On this page "mean" and "average" are the same thing.
Running mean
The mean of the readings you have so far. It changes after every new reading or roll. If you roll a die again and again, the running mean after 10 rolls is the mean of those 10 rolls. After 100 rolls it is the mean of those 100.

3 · How far apart the readings are

Spread
An everyday word, not a number. It means how far the readings are from each other and from the mean. 5, 6, 7 have a small spread. 2, 6, 10 have a large spread. Both have mean 6. "Less spread" means the readings stay closer to the mean.
SD
Standard deviation. The number that measures the spread. It is roughly the typical distance of a reading from the mean. 5, 6, 7: SD = 1. 2, 6, 10: SD = 4. SD has the same unit as the readings (metres for heights, centimetres for diameters). SD = 0 means all readings are the same.
CV
Coefficient of variation. SD as a percentage of the mean: CV = SD ÷ mean × 100. For 5, 6, 7: 1 ÷ 6 × 100 = 16.7 %. CV has no unit, so you can compare the spread of heights (in m) with the spread of diameters (in cm).
Try it: spread and SD. Move the slider. Three readings sit on a number line, always with mean 6. The slider changes how far apart they are. Watch the SD change with them.

4 · How sure we are about the mean

√n
The square root of n. √4 = 2, √9 = 3, √25 = 5, √100 = 10. √3 is about 1.73 and √10 is about 3.16.
SE
Standard error. The SD of a mean. It says how much the mean would typically move if you threw away all the readings and measured n new ones. If you could collect many such means, the SE would be their SD. Formula: SE = SD ÷ √n. For 5, 6, 7: SD = 1, so SE = 1 ÷ √3 = 1 ÷ 1.73 = 0.58. More readings make √n bigger and SE smaller.
95 % range
Two numbers, a low one and a high one, between which the true mean very probably lies. Quick rule: from mean − 2 × SE to mean + 2 × SE. For 5, 6, 7: 6 ± 2 × 0.58 = 6 ± 1.16, so 4.84 to 7.16. Also called a 95 % interval. There is a second way to find it, used later on this page: sort many values, then cut off the lowest 2.5 % and the highest 2.5 %.

5 · Chance

Probability
A number between 0 and 1 that says how likely a result is (see the die and coin above). A fair die: 1/6 for each face. A fair coin: 1/2 for each side.
Share so far
How often a result has actually come up, divided by the number of rolls so far. 21 fours in 100 rolls is a share of 21 %. With many rolls the share gets close to the probability.

Easy to confuse

These twoHow they differ
Spread and SDSpread is the idea: how far apart the readings are. SD is the number that measures it. "Large spread" and "large SD" mean the same thing. You can see spread; you calculate SD.
SD and SESD is the spread of the readings. SE is the spread of the mean: how much the mean would move if you measured everything again. SE is always smaller: SE = SD ÷ √n. For 5, 6, 7: SD = 1 but SE = 0.58. Note that an SE is itself an SD: "SE" is just the special name for the SD of a mean.
SD and CVBoth measure spread. SD has a unit (for example 1 m). CV has no unit, it is a percentage (for example 16.7 %). Use CV to compare two things measured in different units.
Mean and running meanThe mean uses all the readings. The running mean uses the readings so far and keeps changing. When all readings are in, the two are the same.

What this has to do with trees

For the mystery die, the hidden thing is the set of faces and how likely each one is. The rolls are what we get to see. For a tree, the hidden thing is its true height. Each measurement is one "roll": it comes out a little different every time, because of the person measuring, the instrument, the angle and the ground. In both cases we never see the hidden thing directly. We only see readings.

From the readings we make three guesses: a middle (the mean), a spread (the SD), and a range inside which the hidden value very probably lies. And in both cases more readings make the guesses better: the shares settle down, the running mean settles down. The rest of this page builds exactly these three guesses, first with dice, then with a real tree.

After Panel B

Expected value: the long-run average, calculated before rolling

In Panel A the running mean settled at 3.5. In Panel B each mystery die settled at its own number. These numbers can be calculated in advance, from the faces and their probabilities, without rolling at all. The result is called the expected value. It is the long-run average: the number the running mean settles at after many rolls.

Expected value = (value × probability of that value), added up over all the possible values

Every possible value takes part. A value with a high probability pulls the expected value toward itself more strongly. A picture helps: draw the probabilities as bars standing on the number line. The expected value is the balance point, the place where the bars would balance on a pivot (the small triangle).

Die 3, an ordinary die

Faces 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. Each value has probability 1/6.

1 × 1/6 + 2 × 1/6 + 3 × 1/6 + 4 × 1/6 + 5 × 1/6 + 6 × 1/6
= (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) ÷ 6 = 21 ÷ 6 = 3.5

When all values are equally likely, the expected value is simply the plain average of the faces.

Die 1, faces 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 5

Values 1 to 4 have probability 1/6 each. Value 5 is on two faces, so its probability is 2/6. Value 6 has probability 0.

1 × 1/6 + 2 × 1/6 + 3 × 1/6 + 4 × 1/6 + 5 × 2/6 + 6 × 0
= (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 10) ÷ 6 = 20 ÷ 6 = 3.33

The second 5 and the missing 6 pull the balance point down from 3.5 to 3.33. The tall bar at 5 pulls harder than any other bar.

Die 2, faces 1 to 8

Eight values, each with probability 1/8.

1 × 1/8 + 2 × 1/8 + … + 8 × 1/8
= (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 7 + 8) ÷ 8 = 36 ÷ 8 = 4.5

More faces with bigger numbers move the balance point to the right.

Two things to notice. First, "expected" does not mean "the value you expect on the next roll". No face shows 3.5 or 4.5. The expected value is about the average of many rolls, not about one roll. Second, the mean of the rolls (from data) and the expected value (from the probabilities) are two routes to the same number. With few rolls they differ. With thousands of rolls they almost agree. Go back to Panel B and compare "Running mean" with the numbers above.

What this means for readings of a tree

For a die we know the faces, so we can calculate the expected value exactly. For a tree we do not know the "faces": we do not know which readings are possible or how likely each one is. So we cannot calculate the expected value. We can only estimate it, with the mean of the readings we have. If the measuring method is fair, pulling neither up nor down, the expected value of a reading is the true height of the tree. The mean of our readings is our best guess for it, and the more readings, the better the guess.

The same recipe gives the SD from theory: where 1.71 comes from

The SD of the rolls so far in Panel A settles at 1.71. That number can also be calculated in advance, with the same recipe as the expected value. Instead of "value × probability" use "(distance from the expected value)² × probability", add up, and take the square root.

SD from theory = √[ (value − expected value)² × probability, added up over all the possible values ]

The same recipe for 10 rolls of an ordinary die

The rolls were 2, 5, 3, 6, 1, 4, 4, 2, 6, 3. n = 10. Sum = 36. Mean = 36 ÷ 10 = 3.6.

Now compare with what we know about an ordinary die. Its true mean is 3.5 and the SD of one roll is 1.71. Ten rolls gave a mean of 3.6, not exactly 3.5, but the 95 % range 2.52 to 4.68 contains 3.5. The ten rolls did not tell us the exact answer. They gave us a range, and the true value is inside it. That is what readings can do, for a die and for a tree.

Panel C

An average has less spread than a single roll

Now roll k dice at the same time and write down only their average. Compare two cases. One die (k = 1): the result can be anything from 1 to 6, and every value is equally likely. The average of 25 dice (k = 25): the result is usually between 3 and 4, and almost always between 2.8 and 4.2. It practically never goes below 2.5 or above 4.5. That is what "less spread" means: the values stay closer to the middle.

Why does the average stay in the middle? For the average of 25 dice to be 1, every one of the 25 dice must show 1 at the same time. The chance of that is 1/6 × 1/6 × … × 1/6, twenty-five times, which is about 1 in 3 × 1019, practically zero. For the average to be 6, all 25 dice must show 6, equally impossible. Even an average of 2 needs almost every die to show a low number, which is very unlikely. But there are very many ways to get an average near 3.5: some dice show high numbers, some show low numbers, and the high and low ones cancel each other out. So the average of many dice is pulled toward the middle, and its spread is small.

How much less spread? The SD of the averages is the SD of one die divided by √k. The SD of one ordinary die is 1.71, calculated from its six faces in the expected value section above. So with 25 dice (try it below) the SD of the averages is one fifth of 1.71, and with 100 dice it would be one tenth. This rule is the most useful fact in this course.

One number, two names. The SD of the averages has a special name: the standard error, SE. An SE is simply the SD of a mean. Here we can find it in two ways: measure it from the histogram of many averages, or calculate it from the formula SE = SD of one value ÷ √k. The two green boxes below show both. What "95 % confident" means

The interval is for the true height, not for one reading. It answers "where is the hidden value?", not "what will the next student write down?".

"95 %" describes the method, not this one interval. Imagine many classes, each of 250 students, each measuring this tree and each calculating its own interval. The intervals would differ a little, because the readings differ. About 95 out of 100 of those intervals would contain the true height; about 5 would miss it. We have one class and one interval. We cannot know whether ours is one of the 95 or one of the 5. That is why we say "95 % confident", not "certain".

Height is harder to measure than DBH

Height is measured from a distance with an angle reader (a clinometer) and some trigonometry. DBH is measured with a tape touching the trunk. The CV lets us compare the two even though one is in metres and the other in centimetres.

One thing the dice cannot show, and 250 readings cannot fix: if every student's instrument reads 0.5 m too high, the mean is also 0.5 m too high. All the readings would be "rolls" of a die whose faces are shifted. More readings make the interval narrower, but around the wrong value. Only a better instrument, or a check against a tree of known height, can find this kind of error. It is called a systematic error, and we will come back to it when we look at biomass equations.

Panel E

The bootstrap: the 95 % interval without the formula

In Panel D we used a formula: SE = SD ÷ √n, then interval = mean ± 2 × SE. In Panel C we found the SE a different way: by experiment. We rolled the dice thousands of times, made a histogram of the averages, and measured their spread. Can we do the same for the tree? Not directly: we cannot send 2,000 new classes to measure tree #406. We have one bag of 250 readings and nothing else.

The trick. Treat the bag of 250 chips as if it were the tree itself. Drawing one chip from the bag plays the role of measuring the tree once. This is the same idea as in Panel B, where many rolls were a good picture of the die: here, 250 readings are a good picture of what a reading of this tree can look like. So we can "measure the tree" as often as we like, by drawing chips from the bag:

  1. Draw one chip from the bag. Write down its value. Put the chip back.
  2. Repeat 250 times. This is one pretend class of 250 readings. Some chips come out two or three times. Some never come out.
  3. Calculate the mean of the pretend class. Drop it into the histogram.
  4. Repeat steps 1 to 3 thousands of times. The SD of the pretend means is the SE, found by experiment. Sort the pretend means and cut off the lowest 2.5 % and the highest 2.5 %: what remains is the 95 % interval.

Two new words

Bootstrap
The method above: draw chips from the bag of readings you already have, again and again, to make many pretend classes, and measure the spread of their results.
With replacement
Putting the chip back before the next draw, so that the same chip can come out again in the same pretend class. The tree can give the same reading to two students, so the bag must be able to give the same chip twice.

Why use the bootstrap, if the formula gives the same answer?

1. The formula only works for the mean. Press "SD" above. We often want to know how sure we are about the SD, or the CV, or the median, or a biomass calculated from the readings. There is no simple "÷ √n" rule for those. The bootstrap uses the same four steps for any quantity: calculate it for each pretend class and look at the spread.

2. The formula makes an assumption. "Mean ± 2 × SE" assumes that the means of many classes would pile up in a bell shape. Panel C showed that averages do this when the readings are spread evenly around the middle. But if the readings are not symmetric, with a long tail on one side, for example because a few students made very large mistakes, the bell shape is only approximate. The bootstrap makes no such assumption. It uses the actual shape of the readings in the bag.

3. It is one recipe for everything. Draw with replacement, calculate, repeat, sort, cut 2.5 % at each end. The computer does the repeating. We will use exactly this recipe again for the biomass of a whole plot.

What if we did not put the chips back? Then every pretend class would contain exactly the same 250 chips, only in a different order. Every pretend mean would be identical. The histogram would be one single bar, and it would tell us nothing. Putting the chip back is what lets the bag behave like the tree: it can give the same reading twice.

Panel F

Monte Carlo: when a tree has only one reading

In our real project nobody measures one tree 250 times. Each tree is measured once: one height, one DBH. So for a single tree there is no bag of readings to roll. The bootstrap cannot start. A bag with one chip always gives the same answer.

The idea. If we cannot fill the bag with readings, we fill it with an assumption. From Panel D we know how far a height reading can be from the truth: the CV of height readings was about 10 %. (A flat die with limits ± 10 % is not quite the same as a bell die with CV 10 %; see the note at the end of this panel.) So we say: "the true height is somewhere between the reading − 10 % and the reading + 10 %, and we have no reason to prefer any value in that range." That is a die with very many faces, all equally likely, spread evenly from 5.4 m to 6.6 m for a reading of 6.0 m. This shape is called the uniform distribution: drawn as a picture, it has a flat top. Then we roll it, exactly as before. This is the Monte Carlo method, named after the casino town, because it works by rolling dice.

Two new words

Uniform distribution
Every value between a low limit and a high limit is equally likely. An ordinary die is uniform on 1 to 6. Here the die is uniform on "reading − 10 %" to "reading + 10 %".
Monte Carlo
The bootstrap with a bag that we build ourselves from an assumed shape (here uniform) and an assumed size of error (here ± 10 %). Draw, calculate, repeat, sort, cut 2.5 % at each end: the same steps.

A worked example by hand: the volume of a trunk

For one tree alone, Monte Carlo on the height gives nothing new: the mean is the reading and the range is ± 10 %. It becomes useful when the reading goes into a formula. Take the simplest tree formula: treat the trunk as a cylinder. Its volume is V = π × r² × H, where r is the radius, half the DBH.

One tree was measured once: H = 6.0 m and DBH = 8.0 cm, so r = 4.0 cm = 0.04 m. From the readings alone: V = 3.1416 × 0.04 × 0.04 × 6.0 = 0.0302 m³ = 30.2 litres (1 m³ = 1,000 litres). But how sure are we of 30.2? Height can be off by ± 10 %, DBH by ± 3 % (about the size of the DBH spread seen in Panel D). Two errors go into one formula, and the DBH is squared. Working this out with algebra is messy. Rolling is easy.

For a hand calculation, make the two dice with coarse faces. Height die: 13 faces, 5.4, 5.5, 5.6, …, 6.6 m (6.0 ± 10 %). DBH die: 5 faces, 7.8, 7.9, 8.0, 8.1, 8.2 cm (8.0 ± 3 %, rounded to 0.1 cm). Roll both, calculate V, repeat:

Three things to remember about Monte Carlo

The answer is only as good as the assumption. The bootstrap gets its spread from real readings. Monte Carlo gets it from the ± 10 % we put in. Where does the 10 % come from? From an exercise like Panel D, where many people measure one tree; from the instrument's manual; or from a small test. If we assume ± 5 % instead, the interval for V becomes narrower, but not because we measured better. Try it above.

Uniform is the honest choice when you only know the limits. In Panel D the readings piled up in a bell shape, with most readings near the middle. A uniform die says "anywhere between the limits, equally likely". It is the simplest shape, and it is a safe choice when all you know is "about ± 10 %". When more is known, other shapes can be used, for example the bell shape. One care is needed: a flat die with limits ± 10 % is not the same as a bell die with CV 10 %. The flat die from 5.4 m to 6.6 m has an SD of only 0.35 m, a CV of about 6 %, because its values never go beyond the limits. A bell die with CV 10 % has SD = 0.6 m, and about 1 draw in 20 lands more than 1.2 m from the reading. Panel D showed a bell shape with CV about 10 %, so the later pages use the bell die with CV 10 %. This page uses the flat die only because it is easy to roll by hand.

The same dice, a bigger formula. Here the formula was a cylinder with two inputs. For the project, the formula will be the real biomass equation, which has five uncertain inputs instead of two. The method does not change: build a die for each uncertain input, roll them all, calculate, repeat, sort, cut.

What to notice

  1. Probability needs many trials. After 10 rolls, the bars in Panel A are uneven. After 10,000 rolls they are almost flat. "1/6" describes the long run, not the next roll.
  2. Readings let you guess a hidden thing. In Panel B, a few hundred rolls were enough to tell an ordinary die from a die with two fives or from an eight-faced die. In Panel D, 250 readings tell us the height of a tree to within about ± 0.08 m, even though a single reading can be off by more than half a metre.
  3. The same √n rule appears three times. In Panel A, one roll has SD 1.71, so after n rolls the running mean is usually no further than 1.71 ÷ √n from 3.5. For example, after 100 rolls: 1.71 ÷ √100 = 1.71 ÷ 10 = 0.17, so the running mean is usually between 3.33 and 3.67. In Panel C, the SE of the average of k dice is 1.71 ÷ √k. In Panel D, the SE of the mean of 250 readings is SD ÷ √250. It is one rule in three places: averaging n values divides the spread by √n.
  4. Two different 95 % ranges. Mean ± 2 × SD is where 95 % of the single readings fall. Mean ± 2 × SE is where the true value very probably lies. The first is for one roll, the second for the average of many rolls. Do not mix them up.
  5. Two ways to the same interval. Panel D used the formula mean ± 2 × SE. Panel E rolled the bag of readings 2,000 times and got the same interval without any formula. The bootstrap also works for the SD, the median, or anything calculated from the readings, where no formula exists.
  6. Monte Carlo is the bootstrap with a bag you build yourself. When a tree has only one reading, assume the error (for example ± 10 %, uniform), build the die from it, and roll. Panel F turned one height and one DBH into a volume of 30.2 litres with a 95 % interval of about 26.5 to 34.1 litres.
  7. Height is harder to measure than DBH. Press the "DBH, cm" button in Panel D. The DBH readings agree with each other much better (CV about 3 %) than the height readings (CV about 10 %, exactly 10.6 %).
