Quantifying uncertainty in tree biomass · page 3

Monte Carlo, One Tree

What you are looking at. We measured one Pongamia pinnata tree (#406): diameter D = 8.0 cm, height H = 6.0 m, wood density ρ = 0.60 g/cm³. The Chave equation turns these into a biomass of 13.6 kg. But every number going in is a little uncertain, and the equation itself is only approximately right. A Monte Carlo simulation handles this by rolling dice: each "draw" picks a slightly different H, D, ρ, equation and scatter, computes the biomass, and adds it to a pile. After thousands of draws the pile shows the range of answers we should believe.

Median: the middle draw (half the draws are smaller). Mean: the average. SD: standard deviation, the typical distance from the mean. CV % = SD ÷ mean × 100, the "relative error". 95 % interval: from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile; 95 % of draws fall inside it. Numbers need a few hundred draws to settle; by 20,000 they barely move.

One die at a time

Which source matters most? This button repeats the experiment with only one source switched on (5,000 draws each), then all of them together.

Errors from independent sources add "in quadrature" (square, add, square-root), not by simple addition: √(37² + 10² + 10² + 5² + 1²) ≈ 40 %. The biggest source dominates; shrinking a small one hardly helps.

How to report it

A single number hides the uncertainty. Report the middle, the range, and how you got them.

"AGB ≈ 14 kg (95 % interval 6.4–29 kg) from 20,000 Monte Carlo draws; dominated by the model's own scatter."

Why 14 and not 13.6? The point value (13.6 kg) and the median of the draws (≈13.5 kg) agree, but writing "13.6" would suggest we know the answer to 0.1 kg. With a 95 % interval of about 6 to 29 kg, two significant figures are already generous. Round to what you actually know.

Why a 95 % interval and not "± SD"? The pile is not symmetric. "13.6 ± 5.4 kg" would claim the tree could weigh 8 kg as easily as 19 kg, but the true spread runs from 6 to 29. Quoting the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles respects the lopsided shape.

What to notice

  • The equation's own scatter dominates. Even with perfect measurements, the Chave equation is only right to within about ±37 % for a single tree, because real trees of the same D, H and ρ differ in shape and branching. Measuring height more carefully (10 % → 5 %) barely changes the total.
  • Errors combine in quadrature. 37 and 10 and 10 and 5 and 1 do not add to 63; they combine to about 40. Only the biggest terms matter.
  • The pile is lopsided. The mean sits a little above the median and the point value, because a multiplicative error eε can double a tree but can only halve it. Report the median and the percentile interval, not mean ± SD.
  • Monte Carlo is just counting. No calculus, no formulas for error propagation: roll the dice many times and look at the pile. The histogram is the answer.
